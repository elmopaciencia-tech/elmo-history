      The Rise and Fall of Chinese identity in Singapore

      The first generation of Chinese immigrants into Singapore were likely to identify and have had strong emotional ties with their homeland in China.

      As they began to settle in Singapore, it would have been natural to start identifying with their new homeland, especially if they did not plan to return to China1. They became known as the Straits Chinese and occupied a high social status in the country and were often fluent in English.

      However, waves of first-generation immigrants kept coming into Singapore over time. In the late 19th century, many Chinese immigrants arrived into Singapore, known as “Sinkeh Chinese” (新客), and mainly spoke their homeland’s specific dialect2. They were mainly illiterate and uneducated labourers, but they very quickly outnumbered the Straits Chinese. As we had seen earlier, they brought their customs into their new environment to provide some familiarity and to connect with others from the same dialect group. It was not until external developments that truly supercharged the nationalist fervour within the Chinese community.

      "Overseas Chinese"

      The 1911 revolution in China ended 2000 years of imperial rule in China with the victory of the revolutionaries and the overthrow of the Qing dynasty. In its place, the Republic of China was established. In 1929, the government passed the Nationality Act, giving Chinese nationality status to anyone of Chinese descent, regardless of their place of birth3. Many in Singapore’s Chinese community were motivated to reconnect with their Chinese roots during this era.

      Consequently, the Clan associations of the various dialect groups and other powerful Chinese community leaders sought to preserve their Chinese identity by establishing Chinese schools. These schools taught in Mandarin and taught the students Chinese values and the culture. They hoped to foster an ethnic Chinese identity for the next generation. Schools such as Ngee Ann College, Nanyang University and many “public schools” were set up because of these initiatives4.

      Communist Elements

      However, within the vast number of Chinese labourers and students were a strong contingent of communists, which was seen as a threat to the stability of the newly independent Singapore. Chinese schools became breeding grounds for political dissent and its students were behind the organization of several riots and protests.

      Notable incidents they were involved in include the Anti-National Service riots in 1954, the Hock Lee Bus Riots in 1955 and the Singapore Chinese Middle School Students Union riots in 19565. The government also grew more suspicious of opera associations and troupes, as they also harboured suspected communists and leftists6.

      This was not unfounded as leftists did make use of performance arts, including opera, to indoctrinate students into their ideology.

      The PAP cracked down on these activities, and were particularly ruthless against communists as shown in Operation Coldstore in 19637.

      Of the 113 arrested, 40 were trade union leaders and 18 were from educational fields, including students from Nanyang University, Singapore’s only Chinese-medium university.

      Putting down New Roots 落地生根

      In an attempt to forge a Singaporean national identity, the government instituted a bilingual policy in 1966 within government funded schools, mandating English as the medium of teaching but allowing the various ethnicities to learn their ethnic language as a “mother tongue”. For the Chinese however, their mother tongue was Mandarin, not their dialect8.

      Alongside the “Speak Mandarin” campaign initiated by the government in 1979, the prominence and use of dialects, and therefore their identities, declined. By this point, most of the Chinese population had grown up in Singapore and would have identified it as its homeland. Since they were only required to learn Mandarin, each successive generation started to lose fluency in their dialects.

      Meanwhile, the dominance of English in education and media meant that Western pop culture greatly influenced Singapore’s culture, further blurring their Chinese identity. With each new generation raised, educated and socialised in Singapore’s multiracial environment, they progressively detached from their Chinese roots, and increasingly viewed themselves as Singaporean.
